Camera Roll Import

A visual draft of the guided flow that turns a phone full of old photographs into a trip draft. Second pass: restructured around what the research says about wizards, uploads, voice questions and when to ask for money.

Eight things the research changed

v1 was a clean linear wizard. Most of what follows is about the places a linear wizard quietly fails: a task too long for one sitting, an upload that half-succeeds, a question nobody can answer, and a price asked before anybody knows what they're buying.

  • Staged disclosure breaks when steps are interdependentNN/G, progressive disclosure

    Steps 07–09 stopped being a wizard. Picking days, fixing metadata and telling the story are not a sequence — people bounce between them. They are now one workspace you enter per day and leave whenever.

  • A wizard must survive abandonment and re-entrywizard pattern; reminiscence research

    A resume screen, and permission to stop. The interview literature is explicit that memory work goes better over days, not in one sitting. So the flow says so out loud and saves after every answer.

  • People need to know how many steps, where they are, and what happens at the endUSWDS, complex forms

    A persistent progress strip on every screen, and an expectation-setter before the first tap: how long, what you'll need, what you get.

  • Show per-file state, not one bar; make individual failures retryablefile-upload UX

    The upload is a grid of tiles with their own states and a "4 didn't make it — retry those" row. One bar for 120 files hides exactly the failure you need to see.

  • State limits before the choice, not as a rejection after itfile-upload UX

    Limits moved above the picker. What's accepted and how many, before the person has spent four minutes selecting.

  • Specific, sensory questions get stories; open prompts get nothinglife-story interviewing

    Every question rewritten, and a follow-up bank added — what it smelled like, who took the photograph, what happened right after. There's a side-by-side of what was cut.

  • Voice input must expect recognition errors and interruptionvoice UI practice

    The transcript is editable, and pausing is a first-class control. A read-only transcript from a phone in a café is a bug waiting to be published.

  • Delay the price until value is visible; show one real result firstpaywall timing

    One free sample. Before the credits screen, we describe a single photograph the person picks — free — so the offer is a thing they've seen rather than a promise.

The three-line expectation setter

Complex-form guidance is consistent: the structure — how many parts, where you are, what finishing looks like — has to be visible before the first input, or people ration their effort against an unknown.

"You can change this anytime" is worth ~20%

One study of a sign-up field found a single reassurance line cut abandonment by nearly a fifth. The line under the button is doing that job, and it is true.

Privacy said plainly, not linked

"Deleted within two days" and "doesn't count against your storage" are facts about how this works. Putting them behind a link is how people decide to do it later, which means never.

One bar for 120 files hides the thing you need

Upload guidance is blunt about it: show each file's own state, keep the successes when some fail, and make a failed file individually retryable rather than restarting the batch.

Errors say what, why, and what next

"4 didn't make it · the connection dropped · retry those 4" — the three-part shape, in one card, with the fix as a button rather than an instruction.

Chunked, so a retry isn't a restart

A 33 MB clip that fails at 80% must resume, not begin again. On mobile data that difference is whether people finish.

Say the numbers out loud

"104 of 120 already know where they were" is the moment somebody stops bracing for a form. It is also a true statement about their own photographs, which is a different kind of credibility from a progress animation.

Weather is the one field that fills itself

Given a place and a date, the archive reading is fetchable from a real source. Nothing invented, and it is worth saying that it came from somewhere.

A missing place stays missing

No inherited coordinate from a neighbour without the person being told, and never a plausible city name. An empty field beats a confident wrong one.

Step 05 restructured

The days are a workspace, not a queue

This is the biggest change from v1. Choosing a day, fixing its metadata and telling its story are interdependent — people jump between them — and staged disclosure is the wrong pattern for interdependent steps. So the wizard stops here and a board takes over: pick any day, do as much as you like, come back.

There's no hurry, but there is a clock
  • Unused photographs are cleared after two days. Anything already attached to a day is yours and stays.

Memory work wants more than one sitting

Life-story interviewers deliberately spread sessions over days or weeks — people remember more in between. A flow that demands nine days of stories in one go is fighting the way the material actually surfaces.

Re-entry without losing work is the wizard's hardest requirement

So it gets its own screen rather than a toast. It names where you stopped, what's left, and — honestly — how long the held photographs last.

The clock has to be said, not hidden

A 48-hour holding area is fine. A 48-hour holding area somebody discovers by losing their upload is not.

The fields behind the chips

  • Per photograph — place, time of day, who is in it, caption, who may see it. Camera and lens are read and kept but never asked about; nobody wants to be quizzed on a focal length.
  • Per day — the place it happened, a description, the weather, and who may see the day. Weather fills itself once place and date are known.
  • Per trip — title, the people who came, and whether the trip is public, guests-only or private.
Step 07 rewritten

The questions do the work

The interviewing literature is unusually consistent here: open prompts get a sentence, and specific sensory ones get a paragraph. "What could you smell walking through the front door" is the example people cite because it reliably gets twenty minutes. Every question in v2 is built from something the photographs already said.

What got cut, and what replaced it

Opening a day "It's Tuesday morning in Hoi An, it's raining, and you took twelve photographs in about an hour. What were you doing?" "Tell us about this day."
A day with no location "These nine were sent to you rather than taken on your phone, so they don't know where they were. Where were you on the Friday?" "Location missing. Please enter a place."
Getting past the facts "What did it smell like in there?" · "What could you hear?" · "What was the weather actually doing — the reading says light rain." "Add any additional details about this day."
People "Who took this one?" — it names a person without asking anyone to tag a face. "Who is in this photo?"
Closing a day "What happened right after this?" — it either ends the day or opens the next one. "Anything else?"
A day that was nothing "Travel day? Say so and we'll keep it short." — a real, one-tap answer. Forcing a description on a day spent on a bus.

Speech recognition will get the names wrong

Voice guidance treats recognition error as the normal case, not the exception. So the transcript is editable, uncertain words are flagged rather than silently committed, and "say it again" is a button. A read-only transcript is a published mistake.

Interruption is a feature

Pausing is a first-class control, not a cancel. People stop mid-sentence because someone walks in; the flow has to survive that without losing the minute already recorded.

Three questions a day, hard limit

One opener, one gap-filler, one optional follow-up. Nine days at three questions is twenty-seven answers, which is already a lot to ask of somebody's evening.

A taste of the real thing before the ask

The pattern the paywall write-ups keep pointing at: one genuine generated result, free, then the offer. It converts because it stops being a promise — and here it also lets somebody decide the writing isn't for them, which is a fair outcome.

The whole price, up front

Every line priced, the total shown, and the balance after. No toggling to discover a number.

The free path is a real path

"Build it free from what I wrote" produces a complete trip. The credits buy polish on top of the person's own words, which are the point.

Step 10

Read it back, fix anything, then publish

The draft is the trip itself on the site's own pages, not a summary of one. Everything is still editable by hand, and publishing is a second, deliberate press that this flow never makes on anybody's behalf.

What this is standing on, and what it still isn't

  • The iCloud wait and the sleeping screen are measured. Both come from real handset runs — 99 photographs through a live upload, timings recorded. The wait is per-file download, not per-byte transfer.
  • The metadata proportions are real. In one ordinary camera roll, every camera original carried a location and every saved-from-elsewhere image carried none. About one in five photographs will never have a place, and they cluster on the days somebody was sent pictures by the people they were with — which is exactly what Step 07's second question is for.
  • Videos are a known gap. A clip carries its own coordinates and nothing reads them yet. Tracked separately.
  • Still unknown: whether an upload survives being backgrounded, and where the selection ceiling is. Neither changes this shape; both change what Step 03 can promise.
  • Not designed here: the temporary store behind the upload, how credits are priced, and what an instance does when somebody abandons after Phase one. The resume screen assumes an answer to the third that nobody has given yet.
